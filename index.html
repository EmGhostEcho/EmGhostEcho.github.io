Overview

In this paper, we present GhostEcho, the electromagnetic tampering system targeting portable medical ultrasound imaging. GhostEcho injects attacker-controlled artifacts into the ultrasound imaging pipeline via intentional electromagnetic interference (IEMI), requiring no software or network access to the target device. It supports three attack strategies of increasing stealthiness—targeted pattern injection, full-image spoofing, and physically constrained adversarial perturbations imperceptible to human operators. A reconstructed display channel derived from unintentional EM emanations provides real-time visual feedback for adaptive artifact positioning.

Below we provide video demonstrations of the GhostEcho system, including: (1) the experimental setup showing remote EM injection through a wall, (2) feedback-informed injection with localized artifact injection, EM leakage reconstruction, and global image forgery, (3) injection of adversarial perturbations, and (4) a portable attack scenario where all equipment fits inside a laptop bag for covert deployment.

Video Demonstrations

Part 1

Experimental Setup

This video shows the experimental setup. The attacker stands in a room holding an antenna connected to a USRP and an RF power amplifier, transmitting electromagnetic injection signals. The victim ultrasound device is placed in an adjacent room, separated by a wall. Through this remote electromagnetic injection approach, specific interference artifacts appear on the ultrasound imaging display. Notably, the injected artifacts remain clearly visible even when the attacker and the target device are separated by a wall at a distance of several meters.

Part 2

Feedback-Informed EM Injection

GhostEcho establishes a closed-loop attack by capturing EM leakage from the display interface to reconstruct the ultrasound screen in real-time. Using this feedback, the attacker can inject localized artifacts (e.g., circular patterns mimicking renal hamartoma) or replace the entire ultrasound frame with a synthesized forgery. To evaluate the impact on AI-assisted diagnosis, we extracted perturbation patterns from the artifact-injected ultrasound images and applied them to a public kidney stone dataset. The attack success rates reach 72% for ResNet-34, 46% for SqueezeNet 1.1, and 38% for DenseNet-121, demonstrating that localized electromagnetic artifacts can effectively mislead AI classifiers.

Part 3

Adversarial Perturbation

Injection of adversarial perturbation. We investigate whether EM injection can deliver imperceptible perturbations that deceive AI diagnostic models. The universal adversarial perturbation achieves an ASR of 83.6% in digital space and retains 60.6% after physical EM injection, with an SSIM of 0.596 and PSNR of 28.42 dB.

Part 4

Portable Attack Scenario

The attacker can use portable and compact equipment to achieve more covert electromagnetic injection. This video demonstrates that the attacker can perform the EM injection attack using a portable SDR such as HackRF One and a small power supply. All required equipment fits inside a laptop bag, which can be placed near the target ultrasound device without raising suspicion.

Part 5

Testing Other Ultrasound Devices

Beyond the EDAN Acclarix AX3 evaluated in the preceding sections, we further assessed the generalizability of the proposed electromagnetic injection attack across different ultrasound imaging platforms. Specifically, we evaluated the Mindray M9CV, a cart-based ultrasound system clinically deployed in the Cardiac Intensive Care Unit (CICU) of a hospital, where it is routinely employed for inpatient examinations and intraoperative imaging. Our results show that the injected electromagnetic artifacts remain clearly discernible at a separation distance of 3.5 m between the attacking antenna and the target device. Moreover, we demonstrate the injection of localized circular artifacts, including a single circular lesion and a cluster of three circular lesions, confirming that the attack is not limited to a specific product or form factor.
